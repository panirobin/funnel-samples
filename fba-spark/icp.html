01

Fears

What they're privately worried about. None of this gets said on a strategy call — so the copy has to answer it before it's raised.

That the price will die before their units even land

The single most-repeated failure in the category. They buy on today's Buy Box, the units go into a box, and by the time the shipment checks in there are twice as many sellers and the margin is gone.

Heard in the wild
By the time inventory goes live on Amazon, there's more competition and the price has decreased. Source — Seller Assistant, "10 Reasons Why People Fail With Amazon FBA Online Arbitrage"

That their money is stuck in stock that won't move

Arbitrage is cash-in-front. A few bad calls in a row and the float is gone — not lost exactly, just frozen in boxes, which for someone funding this from a salary feels the same.

Heard in the wild
Amazon FBA requires upfront inventory investments, and smart financial planning is needed to ensure expenses can be covered through sales. Source — category post-mortems on why OA sellers fail

That they'll source something they were never allowed to sell

The IP complaint and the account-health score. They've read enough horror stories to be scared of a suspension, and not enough to know which ones are survivable — so they hesitate on everything, which is its own cost.

Heard in the wild
Sourcing products you're not allowed to sell… happens more often than expected, especially to new sellers. Source — Seller Assistant, failure-reason breakdown

That they're simply too late to arbitrage

Retailers limiting bulk buys, Amazon rejecting receipts, brands gating. The quiet suspicion is that the model worked five years ago and they've arrived at the end of it.

Heard in the wild
Retailers are increasingly restricting bulk purchases… Amazon has restricted many top brand names and is rejecting retail receipts as proof of authenticity in some cases. Source — Riverbend Consulting, on arbitrage risk
02

Desires

What they'd actually buy — in their words, not a brochure's. More specific and more modest than you'd expect.

To finish a sourcing session with something bought

Not a hundred leads. Two or three they're confident enough to spend money on, found inside a session that fits in an evening rather than a weekend.

Heard in the wild
Sellers express frustration about spending hours of their time every week "searching" for profitable inventory. Source — sourcing-tool marketing, quoting the pain it sells against

Criteria they can actually apply, not judgement they don't have yet

The thing they envy in an experienced seller is speed of decision. They want thresholds — a number for ROI, a number for offer count, a number for sellers — so the call stops being a feeling.

Heard in the wild
New sellers spend hours analyzing without buying… the fix is to set clear buying criteria and stick to them. Source — OA beginner guides, on analysis paralysis

Reorders instead of a permanent hunt

The dream isn't a bigger lead list. It's the same good product bought again, and again — a catalogue that pays them without another weekend of scanning. This is the desire your own model already answers better than anyone else's.

Heard in the wild
I don't ever buy products that I source… I'd rather rely on my old leads. Those are tested. They're tried and true. Source — Dave Mane, sourcing-processes video (verbatim)
03

Objections

Why they don't buy. These are the four the funnel has to survive — three of them are scars from the last thing they bought.

"I've bought a lead list before. Everyone gets the same list."

The strongest objection in the category, and the easiest to answer honestly: a lead sold to hundreds of people is a lead whose price is already falling. Answer it by refusing to hand out live ASINs at all — teach the scan and show worked, dead examples instead.

Heard in the wild
When using pre-researched deal lists, you're competing with other subscribers buying the same products, which may result in tighter margins. Source — online-arbitrage tooling guides

"I've already watched a hundred hours of free Keepa videos."

True, and it hasn't worked — because watching a filter being built is not the same as having the filter. Meet it with your own line: this is a skill you pick up by implementing, not by watching modules.

Heard in the wild
This is not a skill you're going to learn today just by watching this video. Source — Dave Mane, sourcing-criteria video (verbatim)

"I don't have the capital to test."

Don't argue with it — it's usually real. Answer it with the five-to-ten-unit test buy, which is how you talk about slow movers anyway, and with the fact that killing bad leads faster is the cheapest thing they can change.

Heard in the wild
If you're new to Amazon FBA arbitrage, start with a small quantity of units. Source — OA beginner guides

"Is arbitrage even still viable?"

Answer with the sustainable-model framing rather than a pep talk: the people struggling are the ones starting from zero every month. The ones who are fine are re-buying tested leads. That reframe is the offer.

Heard in the wild
The bar for staying successful in arbitrage has risen, requiring consistent sourcing routines and a solid understanding of Amazon fees. Source — category guides, 2026 outlook
04

Buying triggers

The moment the problem stops being tolerable. Ads that name the trigger beat ads that name the solution — the trigger is what they're already thinking about.

A lead that just went bad

Units bought, price collapsed, money stuck. The sting is recent and specific, and they want to know which signal they missed. This is the single highest-intent moment in the market.

An IP complaint or account-health email

Nothing focuses a seller like a notice from Amazon. They go looking for someone who can tell them whether it's survivable — and whether they could have seen it coming on the chart.

A whole weekend of sourcing with nothing bought

Six hours, forty tabs, zero purchases. It's the moment they stop blaming the market and start looking for a method.

Getting stuck at the filter screen

They watched a teardown, opened Product Finder, and couldn't reproduce it. The gap between watching and doing is exactly where a live workshop converts.

05

Their vocabulary

The words this market uses for its own problem. Written in these, copy reads as insider; written around them, it reads as an outsider guessing.

sourcing a lead that tanked offer count the pink line Buy Box rotation suppressed Buy Box IP complaint account health ROI sell-through replen dead stock product research passive income e-commerce store

Struck-through terms are ones to avoid — they say sourcing, not "product research"; "passive income" marks you as selling to beginners who won't buy; and nobody in this market calls it an "e-commerce store", they say my store or my catalogue.

06

Where they are

Where this audience already gathers and what they read — useful for targeting, and for knowing which references land.

Reddit — r/AmazonSeller, r/FulfillmentByAmazon, r/AmazonFBAOnlineRetail

Where the post-mortems live: tanked leads, suspension appeals, "is OA dead in 2026". Deeply sceptical of anything that looks like a guru pitch, and very responsive to specific numbers.

YouTube and TikTok sourcing teardowns — including yours

Long-form screen-shares are how this market learns. The comment sections are full of people who watched, tried it, and got stuck — which is precisely the workshop's audience and the cheapest traffic you have.

Keepa, Seller Amp and repricer communities

Tool-adjacent groups and Discords. People here already pay for software, which makes them far easier to sell a paid solution to than a cold beginner.
