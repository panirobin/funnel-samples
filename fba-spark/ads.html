Hook

You just spent six hours in Keepa and bought nothing. Forty tabs open, half of them still loading, and not one lead you'd actually put money on.

That's not a you problem and it's not because Amazon is picked over. It's that you're giving every listing the same twenty minutes — including the ones that were never going to work.

Body

I'm Dave. I've been reselling on Amazon for five years and I've spent thousands of hours doing exactly what you're doing. The thing that actually changed my numbers wasn't a better way to find winners. It was a faster way to throw out losers.

There are four things I look at before I read anything else on a listing. If any one of them is there, I'm out — not "I'll think about it", out, in about two seconds. An IP complaint anywhere in the history. The brand sitting in the Buy Box. A price spike that isn't what it looks like. And an offer count that's been climbing all month. That last one is the one that gets people: the price hasn't crashed yet, but it's going to.

The four kill signals and how to read them off a chart at a glance. The 10% rule — why a lead that isn't close enough at list price is never getting there with coupons, so you stop burning an hour on it. And live, on screen: a hundred-lead list cut down to the handful worth opening.

Hook

Every month you start over. New scan, new leads, new gamble. And if you stop sourcing for two weeks, the business stops.

That's not a business yet. That's a job you gave yourself.

Body

I'm Dave. Here's something that surprises people: I rarely source for my own store anymore. I source with students every day, but for my own catalogue I'd rather go back to leads I've already tested — the ones I know sell, at prices I know I can hit.

That's the whole shift. A lead you bought once and did well on is worth more than a new lead you've never tested, because you already know how it behaves. How to set a price alert on a lead so the reorder finds you instead of you hunting for it. What to record about a lead so re-buying takes thirty seconds, not a research session. And why a product that survived a wave of competition is usually the safest thing you'll ever buy again.
