01

Fears

What they're privately worried about. None of this gets said on a discovery call — so the copy has to answer it before it's raised.

That the accent is quietly capping their career

They suspect people rate them as less sharp than they are, and that it costs them the interview, the promotion, the room's attention — without anyone ever saying so.

Heard in the wild
Implicit biases can label people with foreign accents as less competent. Source — essays on accent bias in hiring

Fine in person, falls apart on the phone

Face-to-face they're understood. On a phone screen, a Zoom, a recorded demo — no lip-reading, no body language — the same sentences stop landing, and the highest-stakes conversations are exactly the remote ones.

Heard in the wild
My accent is easily understood in face-to-face conversations… but I struggle during phone screening interviews. Source — TeamBlind, job-search thread

That fixing it means erasing where they're from

The word "reduction" sits badly. They don't want to sound like they're ashamed of their first language — so they put the whole project off rather than sit with that feeling.

Heard in the wild
Leave my accent alone. Source — personal essay, second-language speaker
02

Desires

What they'd actually buy — in their words, not a brochure's. More specific and more modest than you'd expect.

Be answered on the point, not the pronunciation

They want the listener thinking about what they said, not working to decode it. The tell they hate most is the small unfocusing of the eyes mid-sentence.

Heard in the wild
Each time I spoke I caught the faintest squint in the interviewer's eyes — a sign of not understanding. Source — TeamBlind, interview thread

Stop repeating themselves

Not perfect speech — just not having to say the sentence twice, and not bracing for "sorry, could you say that again?" on every call.

Heard in the wild
My accent has always been a conversation stopper… I occasionally get tired of repeating myself. Source — essay, Slavic-accent speaker

Sound like themselves on demand — not just reading aloud alone

They can already hit the sound when they concentrate. What they want is for it to hold live, when they're improvising and thinking about the content instead of their mouth.

Heard in the wild
I want to sound clear and confident, not just when I practise by myself. Source — composite, accent-coaching forum threads
03

Objections

Why they don't buy. Pulled from sceptical threads and from Dillon's own FAQ, where he answers these directly.

"I've already done the apps and a course — nothing stuck"

Answer it honestly: they're right that passive study rarely transfers. The workshop's whole premise is that understanding a sound and keeping it under pressure are different skills, and only the second one is the gap.

Heard in the wild
Self-study, choosing cheap options like AI apps… those take a long time to bring about any meaningful change. Source — accentchange.com, FAQ

"Won't this just make me sound fake?"

The identity fear again, as a buying objection. Meet it with Dillon's own line — changing the sounds didn't make him less German — and the fact that the target is General American, not an impression.

Heard in the wild
…the same methods actors use to prepare for big roles. Without sounding fake. Source — Dillon Ford, LinkedIn

"I don't have time to practise every day"

Don't argue with it. The drill is built to run in ten minutes, and Dillon is explicit that he doesn't expect daily perfection from working clients.

Heard in the wild
I don't get mad at all when my students tell me they haven't had time to practice that week. Source — SHOUTOUT LA interview
04

Buying triggers

The moment the problem stops being tolerable. Ads that name the trigger beat ads that name the solution — the trigger is what they're already thinking about.

A specific high-stakes moment on the calendar

A fundraise, a conference talk, a big client demo, a promotion interview, a podcast booking — something where the accent is about to be in front of an audience that matters.

One call that went badly on a single word

A product name, their own company name, a phrase with a TH in it — misheard on a live call, and the whole conversation wobbled. The specific, recent sting.

Realising months of study changed nothing

They finish a course or a stretch of app streaks and notice not one habit actually moved. Theory piled up; delivery didn't. That gap is the opening.

05

Their vocabulary

The words this market uses for its own problem. Written in these, copy reads as insider; written around them, it reads as an outsider guessing.

the accent barrier sound like yourself the squint asked to repeat reverting under pressure the R / the TH / vowels drilling General American fluent but not clear accent elimination ESL broken English

Struck-through terms are ones to avoid — "elimination" reads as erasure, "ESL" is for people still learning the language (this audience is already fluent), and "broken English" is an insult, not a description.

06

Where they are

Where this audience already gathers and what they read — useful for targeting, and for knowing which references land.

Reddit — r/languagelearning, r/accents, r/EnglishLearning

Peer advice and a lot of "is it even possible to change this at my age." Practical, sceptical of quick fixes, allergic to anything that sounds like a pitch.

LinkedIn and immigrant-founder / operator communities

Where the career-cost framing lives — posts about interviews, promotions, being talked over. Also the comment sections under accent-coach YouTube channels, Dillon's included.
