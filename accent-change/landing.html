// What we'll cover

Here's what we'll get through in 52 minutes

Three parts, each building on the last. You don't leave with a phonetics chart — you leave with a drill you can run tomorrow morning in ten minutes.

Part 01 First third

Why the sounds slip when it counts

  • The three sounds — the American R, the TH, the short vowels — that give away most foreign accents on an English call, and why they're the first to collapse under pressure.
  • The real gap: you understand the sound, but understanding was never a habit. In a live conversation there's too much going on to catch it in time.
  • A 40-second self-test: record one sentence calm, one sentence rushed, and hear the exact moment the accent comes back.
Part 02 Middle third

Drill it until the call can't shake it

  • What a real drill looks like — short reps that build the sound into muscle memory, not another explainer video you watch once and nod along to.
  • How “done right” feels: the sound holds when you're tired, interrupted, or thinking about the deal instead of your mouth.
  • The recording loop that tells you it's sticking — so you know you're improving without a coach in the room.
Part 03 Final third

Live simulations, on your sentences

  • We run real pressure-tests from the room — a cold open, a hard question, a price objection — and fix the sound in real time.
  • The most common mistake: speeding up to get past the hard word, which is the exact moment it breaks.
  • Open Q&A: your accent, your situation, answered directly.

// The difference

What changes after these 52 minutes

The problem was never your English. It's that understanding a sound and keeping it under load are two different skills — and only the second one needs a room, a script, and someone pushing back.

Without the workshop

  • You know exactly how the American R works — and still miss it the second a prospect pushes back.
  • You sound clean reading a script alone, then mushy the moment the call goes off-script.
  • People catch 90% of what you say and quietly stop asking about the other 10%.
  • You've done the apps and a course. You have notes, not a single changed habit.
  • So you try to “just speak more” — and drill the old sounds in deeper.

With the workshop

  • The R holds when the question is hard, because you drilled it past the point of thinking about it.
  • Off-script sounds the same as on-script — the habit doesn't care whether you're improvising.
  • People respond to the point you made, not the word they had to stop and decode.
  • You leave with a drill you can run in ten minutes, not a folder of theory to get to later.
  • You practise the few reps that actually transfer — and stop reinforcing the accent you're trying to change.
